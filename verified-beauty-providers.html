Overview

A marketplace where verification is the product

Verified Beauty Providers is a three-sided beauty & wellness marketplace — customers, service providers, and training providers — built on a single promise: every listing is verified. This case study follows the customer side of that promise: from a first search to a confirmed booking, translated from a real Figma system into a live, token-accurate prototype.

Before this, the customer hub was only a placeholder screen — "waiting on Figma." This became the first real product built from the actual Figma system, section by section, on top of the existing token layer.

The problem

You can't see verification

A customer choosing a beauty or wellness professional online has no easy way to tell a genuinely qualified provider from an unverified listing. "Verified" is the whole value proposition — but a checkmark buried on a profile does nothing for a customer scanning a results page.

So the design problem wasn't "show a badge." It was: make verification legible at every step — on the card before you click, on the detail page before you book, and in the loop that brings you back.

Approach

Build from the real system, stay honest about the gaps

  • Build directly from the Figma design, section by section — not a re-interpretation.
  • Use the existing token layer rather than inventing new values, so the prototype stays reconcilable against the source.
  • Stay transparent wherever the source itself was incomplete — reproduce placeholders faithfully, flag inconsistencies, don't silently "fix" things that were never specified.

The work

Discover → evaluate → commit → confirm

A customer either books a service or enrolls in a course — structurally different products (one scheduled time with a provider, the other enrollment in a program) that follow the same shape.

Trust signal · 1

Ratings & reviews

A 1–5 star score plus review count on every listing card, with a full breakdown and individual reviews on the detail page.

Why: it's the first trust signal a customer sees — surfaced before any other detail loads.

Trust signal · 2

Verified marks & accreditation

Verified-provider marks and accreditation logos on the detail page's Company Profile section — the specifics behind the "verified" claim, not just a checkmark.

Why: trust needs receipts; a badge alone is easy to distrust.

Trust signal · 3

Accessibility as a primary filter

Wheelchair accessible, women-only and LGBTQ+ friendly sit as primary filter checkboxes on the listing page — alongside service type and location.

Why: for the people who need these, they're not "advanced" options — they're the decision.

Trust signal · 4

Offers, before the click

Active discounts and "book X, save Y%" offers shown directly on listing cards and reiterated on the detail page — visible before a customer commits to opening a profile.

Why: no bait-and-switch; the deal is part of the first impression.

The provider detail page — where the badge becomes a booking

Everything a customer needs to convert trust into a decision, on one screen — no second page required to answer "can I trust this place":

  • Current offers pinned near the top, then a full service menu with per-treatment pricing — no "contact for pricing" dead ends.
  • A gallery of past work — visual proof alongside the written credentials.
  • Named staff bios with roles and credentials — trust attached to real people, not just the business.
  • An accreditations & awards section, an amenities icon grid, and a location map.

The retention loop

A rewards system, not a coupon

Post-booking is where a marketplace either compounds or leaks. The dashboard hub pulls the whole loop into one place — bookings, wishlist, payment history, and four reward mechanics that reinforce each other:

  • Verified Beauty Points — accrue per booking, redeemable against future bookings, surfaced as a persistent sitewide banner, not buried in account settings.
  • Loyalty Points — tracked in a dedicated dashboard module.
  • E-Gift Cards — buy, send or redeem for yourself or someone else, managed from the same dashboard — reinforcing the same loop rather than a separate feature.
  • Invite-a-Friend — a referral code shareable from the dashboard, with rewards on both sides.

Post-booking states are designed too. Rescheduling gets its own "Consider Rescheduling" prompt before a cancellation is finalized, and cancellation captures a reason rather than silently deleting the booking.

Design-system discipline

The hard part wasn't the visuals

On a system this large, shortcuts would be easy to take and hard to notice. The discipline was resisting the urge to "fix" or invent where the Figma source was incomplete — while still catching and documenting the real inconsistencies.

Reconciliation

Tokens, not reinvention

Every colour on the landing page pulled from the real token layer — the Figma style guide's variable definitions — not approximated or eyeballed.

Caught & flagged

An inconsistency, documented

The landing page's real button component uses square corners — diverging from the pill-shaped button built earlier from the style guide. Documented as a gap to reconcile, not silently overridden.

Fidelity

Faithful to incomplete source

Where Figma's own components still used one repeated placeholder, the build reproduced that faithfully instead of fabricating distinct copy that was never actually specified.

Verify the tool

Cross-checked before skipping

A metadata read initially showed two sections as empty. It was a tooling quirk, not missing content — caught by cross-checking against a full context pull before skipping anything.

Typography & colour

Headings in Advice Medium Lys, body and UI text in Montserrat. The palette is the real token layer — pulled directly from the Figma style guide's variable definitions, not approximated.
