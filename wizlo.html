Overview

What Wizlo is

Wizlo (SmarterEMR) is a SaaS platform for clinics — an electronic medical record (EMR) system that also runs the business side of a practice. It spans three audiences that rarely see each other's screens: patients, clinic admins, and providers / clinical staff.

The product covers the full lifecycle of a patient relationship: a lead comes in, fills out intake forms, gets a cost estimate reviewed for compliance, has a clinical encounter (in person or virtual), starts treatment, buys subscriptions or bundled programs, and receives ongoing notifications through a portal that each clinic can brand as their own.

I lead UX across that whole lifecycle — from early flows and information architecture through production-ready components — working alongside product and engineering.

The problem

Complex because the business is complex

Clinical software is usually judged on how well it handles charts and appointments. Wizlo's harder problem is healthcare revenue cycle: the sequence of estimates, approvals, licensing checks, subscriptions and billing-adjacent orders that decides whether a clinic actually gets paid — and stays compliant while doing it.

Two forces made this a genuinely difficult design problem:

  • Regulation lives in the UI. A Good Faith Estimate (GFE) — a written cost estimate US clinics must give patients before treatment — has to be created, reviewed, and reconciled against what actually happens in the encounter. The compliance rules can't sit in a policy doc; they have to be modelled into states, permissions and handoffs on screen.
  • Every role wants a different thing from the same object. A provider wants operational control over their queue. A patient wants a calm, legible experience and no surprises. An admin wants configurability. The same encounter or estimate has to serve all three without becoming a wall of fields.

My job was to take a process that's complicated because the business is complicated — and make the interface simple anyway.

Approach

How I worked

  • Model the domain first. Before screens, I mapped each object (lead, estimate, encounter, subscription, order, notification) with its real states and the transitions between them, so the UI could be built on top of an accurate model rather than a hopeful one.
  • Design the pattern, not the screen. Wizlo has many similar-but-not-identical flows (review queues, assignment logic, template → preview → publish). I designed reusable patterns for those and let each module inherit them.
  • Prototype to pressure-test. Figma and HTML/CSS prototypes to walk product and engineering through the multi-role handoffs before a line of production code was written.
  • Build close to the metal. I implement Wizlo's UI directly — writing HTML/CSS and React components documented in Storybook, with handoff through Git/GitHub — so what I design is what actually ships.

The work

Six workstreams across the lifecycle

Each of these shipped as its own module on top of the shared patterns. Grouped roughly in the order a patient moves through the product.

Lead → Intake

Lead Management & Forms Builder

A configurable, multi-step intake flow that clinics assemble themselves — logo, colour and typography — so each clinic's first impression feels like their own product, not a vendor's.

Key decision: progressive multi-form progression so long intakes don't feel like a wall; branding tokens that flow straight into the patient portal.

WZ-6776 · Encounter

Sync Encounter

The live side of care: a manual queue, a virtual waiting room, provider availability, and round-robin assignment logic — deciding who sees whom, and when.

Key decision: give providers real operational control over the queue while keeping the waiting patient's view calm and low-anxiety — two very different UIs on the same event.

GFE → Encounter

GFE Review → Encounter handoff

Reviewer assignment, synchronous vs. asynchronous review flows, and licence validation — the point where a Good Faith Estimate becomes an actual encounter, with revenue-cycle and compliance rules modelled directly into the states and permissions.

Key decision: make the compliance path the default happy path, not an extra gate — so doing it right is the fastest route.

WZ-1999 · Portal

Patient & White-Label Portal

Patient journeys, treatment timelines and appointments — with per-brand customization so individual healthcare brands could white-label the whole portal.

Key decision: one timeline component that reads clearly whether a patient has one appointment or a year-long program.

Orders

Subscriptions & Product Bundles

Plans, bundles, and treatment / program packages, plus a product-level timeline for orders that span multiple treatments over time.

Key decision: treat a multi-treatment program as a single object with a timeline, rather than a pile of separate orders the admin has to mentally stitch together.

WZ-2422 · WZ-7597

Clinic Portal & Notifications

Plan-creation and subscription-management workflows for admins, plus patient / provider notification configuration across Email and SMS — with template, preview, draft and publish states.

Key decision: a single template → preview → draft → publish pattern reused everywhere something gets sent, so admins learn it once.

Nova Design System

The system underneath all of it

Six modules built by a small team would drift fast without a shared foundation. I built and maintain the Nova Design System — the tokens, colour, typography and reusable components that every Wizlo module is assembled from.

What made it work in practice:

  • Tokens first. Colour, type and spacing as tokens, so a clinic's white-label branding is a token swap rather than a redesign.
  • Patterns, not just parts. The queue, the assignment picker, the template → preview → publish flow — documented as patterns so new modules inherit behaviour, not just styling.
  • AI-assisted, end to end. I use AI-assisted workflows to move from Figma to production: components written in HTML/CSS and React, documented in Storybook, shipped through Git/GitHub. It closes the loop between the design file and the running frontend.

Qualitatively, the wins I'd point to:

  • The compliance-critical GFE path became the default flow, not a bolt-on — reviewers move through it faster and there's less room to skip a step.
  • New modules now start from Nova patterns, so the team ships consistent multi-role UI without re-litigating the queue or the publish flow each time.
  • Because I implement the components, design intent survives the handoff — what's in Figma is what's in the product.

What I'd do differently

Reflection

  • Model earlier, in the open. The state maps paid off — but a couple of modules were partway designed before the model was solid, and reworking them cost time. Now the state model is the first artifact, shared with engineering before any screens.
  • Write the pattern docs as I go. Nova's patterns got documented in bursts. Documenting each one the moment it stabilised would have saved onboarding friction later.
  • Get the multi-role prototype in front of real providers sooner. The provider / patient split on the encounter was right, but I'd validate that tension with actual clinical staff earlier rather than reasoning it through.
